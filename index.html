Touch

Sensory discriminative touch

Responsible for detecting discriminatory touch such as pressure, vibration, slip, texture.

Typically there are both glarborus and hairy fibers that will detect touch, as they are used to encode for different spatial and temporal properties of surfaces and objects (McGlone et al., 2014). ALpha beta afferents are rapid large myelinated fibers that help with this such as meisssner, and pacinian corpuscles that are responsible for spatial movement stimulus and merkel and ruffini endings that detect constant mechanical stimulus (McGlone et al., 2014).

What is touch?

Touch is a very early sense that occurs in fetal development starting as early as 8 weeks in gestational age.

Maturation of being able to distinguish between motivational-affective and sensory-discriminative tactile stimuli can be differentiated at 35-37 weeks of gestation (Fabrizi et al., 2011). Low threshold mechanoreceptors (LTM) are cutaneous senses that have sub modalities of pressure / vibration, temperature, itch and pain. We will focus on two dimensions of this; Sensory discriminative which highlights the localization of the stimuli on the skin and being able to explore textures, pressure and properties of objects. Secondly, the motivational-affective which highlights the emotional experience of the touch. More specifically, affective touch is a specialized tactile stimulation occurring during gentle, slow rhythmic stroking typically impacting the role of emotional bonding and connection (La Rosa et al; McGlone et al., 2014).

Motivational Affective touch

Responsible for transmitting touch into emotional contexts.

C-tactile afferents are located on the outer boundary of the nervous system and transmits information through the hairy fibers to the brain like affective touch, pain, pleasure, fear. THe development of the C-tactile fibers begin at a very early age; at 25 weeks gestational age, there are sown to be neural reactions at the somatosensory cortex. Already by the third trimester, the fetus shows to respond through kicking during maternal touch on the abdomen (Croy et al., 2022). C-tactile fibers have a smaller diameter creating a slower conduction velocity, the response from these fibers can be activated through a low force, and a temperature close to human skin measured around 32 degrees celsius. (La Rosa et al., 2024). Working together with the alpha beta fiber; when a touch is sensed via the c fibers, alpha beta fibers will activate targeting the somatosensory corticies, while the c tactiles target the posterior insular cortex and dorsoanterior cingulate cortex, both important for the emotional systems (Croy et al., 2022; McGlone et al., 2014).

Skin-to-Skin Tactile Stimulation

Skin to skin contact stimulation begins early even in the womb when fetuses are seen to be reacting to maternal touch from the outside abdomen (Marx & Nagy, 2017). Post birth, mothers are encouraged to have skin to skin contact with their child as it shows to improve autonomic functioning and enhance child development (Feldman et al., 2014).

Mechanism

Activation of C-tactile afferents during gentle touch promotes oxytocin release, reducing stress and promoting growth.

C-tactile afferents are specialized nerve fibers that respond specifically to pleasant, gentle touch, sending signals directly to the brain's social processing centers.

Regulation

Helps regulate infant heart rate, breathing, and temperature immediately after birth.

This "co-regulation" between parent and child provides a biological safety net that stabilizes the infant's still-developing autonomic nervous system.

Bonding

Establishes an essential secure attachment foundation between caregiver and infant.

The release of oxytocin during skin-to-skin contact is often called the 'love hormone' and is crucial for creating a lifelong emotional bond.

Physiological Effects

Early positive tactile experiences are linked to better attention regulation in childhood.

Children who received higher levels of affectionate touch as infants show improved problem-solving skills and higher resilience in stressful environments.

Social Skills

Reduced cortisol reactivity in infants who receive consistent comforting touch.

Lower baseline cortisol levels prevent the negative effects of chronic stress on the developing brain.

Emotional Regulation

Enhanced synaptic connections in somatosensory cortex regions.

The brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to sensory input is at its peak during the first year of life.

Dyad Impacts

Mothers

Infants develop an understanding of their body's position in space through movement.

By feeling their own limbs and receiving external touch, infants create a "cortical map" of their physical self.

Pre-term Infants

Proprioceptive feedback is essential for reaching, grasping, and eventual crawling.

The sensory feedback loop between touch and movement is the primary way infants learn to coordinate complex motor patterns.

Neural Pathways

Sensory input stimulates the formation of new neural connections.

Each touch sends an electrical signal that strengthens the path between neurons, making future communication faster.

Synaptic Pruning

Frequently used connections are strengthened while unused ones are eliminated.

This process makes the brain more efficient by focusing resources on the senses and skills that the infant uses most.

Myelination

Fatty sheaths coat nerve fibers, speeding up impulse transmission.

Consistent sensory stimulation promotes the growth of myelin, which is essential for rapid brain-body communication.

Plasticity

The infant brain is highly adaptable to environmental experiences.

This window of opportunity is why early intervention and positive stimulation are so critical for long-term health.
